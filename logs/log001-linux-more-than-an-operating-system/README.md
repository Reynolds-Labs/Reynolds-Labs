# LOG-001 — Linux: More Than an Operating System

### My Journey Back to Linux

**Reynolds Labs | The Logs — LOG-001**  
**Published:** November 9, 2026  
**Categories:** [About Me](../README.md#about-me) • [Perspectives](../README.md#perspectives)

---

My first real encounter with Linux happened in college. I attended college a little later in life, so I already knew Linux existed as an operating system separate from Windows and macOS, but I didn't know much beyond that.

That changed when I enrolled in a Linux server administration class.

I immediately connected with it.

I loved discovering the different Linux distributions and, more importantly, the amount of control you had over how a Linux system was built and configured. There was something fascinating about being able to take an operating system and shape it around what you needed rather than simply accepting what was provided.

It was a great class, and one thing I particularly appreciated was how the instructor structured our assignments.

We started with basic server management, but each lab built upon the previous one. I thought that was brilliant. You couldn't simply copy your way through an assignment or take shortcuts without consequences. If you didn't properly configure something in an earlier lab, there was a good chance your next lab wouldn't work.

You had to understand what you were building because you would eventually depend on it.

Looking back, that approach to learning probably influenced how I think about technology more than I realized at the time.

## When Life Takes You in Another Direction

By the time I graduated from college, my immediate goal was pretty straightforward: **find a job that would get my foot in the door of the IT industry.**

When you're starting your career, you naturally focus on the skills and experience that will help you land that first opportunity. You build your résumé around the foundation you've developed and the technologies employers are looking for.

Gradually, my interest in Linux took a back seat.

I did land a job, and I hit the ground running, using my Windows experience and troubleshooting skills to join the never-ending fight against IT problems.

As the years went on, my fondness for Linux and open-source applications went into hibernation.

It never really disappeared. It just wasn't at the top of my priority list.

Then, one day, we were making a design change at work, and I found myself once again staring into the mouth of the animal known as Linux.

And just like that, an old interest started waking up.

Returning to Linux felt a little like riding an old, familiar bike with a funny name. Some things had changed, and I certainly had some catching up to do, but the familiarity was still there.

I started building test environments and experimenting with a small home lab. Nothing elaborate at first, just a couple of virtual machines and the curiosity to see what I could accomplish.

Over time, those experiments grew.

I also noticed something interesting about my habits. Whenever I needed an alternative to a commercial application or wanted to explore a different way of accomplishing something, I frequently found myself returning to the Linux and open-source ecosystem.

I kept going back to the same well.

## The Windows 11 Crossroads

Then came the big push toward Windows 11.

My personal laptop, which had been with me since college, was approaching the end of its useful life as a Windows machine. At the same time, I was looking at the prices of new computers and finding it difficult to justify spending that much money on hardware that didn't seem particularly impressive.

That left me with a decision.

Do I purchase a new Windows 11 computer for my home lab?

Or do I finally take the plunge and go fully Linux?

At that point, I wasn't nearly as comfortable with Linux as I am today. I spent some time weighing the advantages and disadvantages.

I knew Linux could do what I needed, but knowing something is possible and committing to using it as your primary environment are two different things.

Eventually, I decided to go for it.

I installed Ubuntu on my aging laptop, and suddenly, that old machine had a new lease on life.

I had expected some compromises. I figured there would be applications I missed, features I preferred in Windows, or little annoyances that might leave me questioning the decision.

I was prepared for at least a little buyer's remorse.

**Nope. Not at all.**

Within a month, I had rebuilt my home lab into something better than what I had before. I established a secondary testing environment and found myself spending more and more time experimenting with Linux.

Configuring systems, securing environments, automating tasks, troubleshooting problems, and occasionally breaking something just to figure out how to put it back together.

What started as a way to extend the life of an aging laptop became something much bigger.

And I haven't looked back.

## The Freedom to Build

One of the things I've always loved about Linux is that it gives you the tools to create, design, and build what you want.

The possibilities are enormous.

Want a lightweight workstation? Build one.

Need a reliable file server? Configure one.

Want to automate a tedious task, experiment with virtualization, or host an open-source application? There are tools available to help you accomplish those things.

Of course, that freedom comes with a catch.

**It depends on how much work you're willing to put into it.**

Linux doesn't always hand you a perfectly packaged solution. Sometimes you have to research, experiment, troubleshoot, and spend a considerable amount of time figuring out how the pieces fit together.

But to me, that's part of the appeal.

There's something satisfying about starting with a problem, exploring the available tools, and eventually creating a solution that works exactly the way you intended.

And sometimes the solution you discover isn't even close to the one you originally imagined.

## Linux Isn't Always the Answer

Now, as much as I enjoy Linux, I'm not going to pretend it's the perfect operating system for everyone.

I'd love to say everyone should switch to Linux and never look back.

But that simply isn't realistic.

Technology decisions need to make sense for the people using the technology, not just the people administering it.

For example, imagine an office full of employees who rely on Windows and Microsoft Office to accomplish their daily work.

Would I recommend suddenly replacing their computers with Linux and switching everyone to LibreOffice simply because I personally enjoy open-source software?

**Absolutely not.**

That sounds like a recipe for a full-blown technology disaster for both the employees and the IT department.

The disruption, training requirements, application compatibility concerns, and potential loss of productivity could easily outweigh whatever advantages the change might offer.

But suppose that same organization needs a reliable file server, a dedicated application host, or an open-source utility that can solve a particular business problem.

Now Linux might be an excellent option.

That's how I believe technology should be approached.

Not as a competition to determine which operating system is universally superior, but as an opportunity to select the right tools for the job.

Windows has its place. Linux has its place. And sometimes the best environment is one where they work together.

## A Hundred Ways to Solve a Problem

One of my favorite things about working in technology is that it never stops growing or changing.

The problems evolve. The tools evolve. The way we approach solutions evolves right along with them.

And I think that's ultimately what keeps drawing me back to Linux.

There's rarely just one way to accomplish something.

You might find five different tools that solve the same problem, each approaching it a little differently. You might discover a solution someone else developed years ago, or you might decide to build something yourself because the existing options don't quite fit.

Sometimes you'll spend hours troubleshooting an issue only to discover that the answer was surprisingly simple.

Other times, what seemed like a simple project turns into an entirely new learning experience.

That's technology.

And that's what makes it interesting.

**With Linux, there are a hundred different ways to change something, build something, or solve a problem.**

You just have to be willing to explore, figure out what works, and make it happen.

For me, that's the fun of it.

And it's a big part of why Linux has become more than just another operating system.
